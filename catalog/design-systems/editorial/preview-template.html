The persistence of the long page

For ten years, every prediction has said the web would shrink to the size of a tweet. Instead the long-form essay survived — and not by accident.

Mira Allende · Contributing editor 21 May 2026 · Reading time 17 min

The argument against the long-form essay has been made every spring for a decade. The attention span is shrinking. The form is dying. The reader has migrated to vertical video. Every prediction has been wrong, and the longform essay has not just survived — it has become, somehow, the most-trusted unit of writing on the open internet.

Part of the explanation is structural. The long page is the only writing that pays the reader back at the rate the reader wants to be paid. The tweet rewards the writer; the carousel rewards the platform; the essay rewards the reader. When the algorithmic feeds collapsed into one another in 2024 and 2025, the place that held its shape was the slow page that respects the reader's time as a real cost.

The long page rewards the reader.
The short post rewards the writer. The reader, eventually, notices the difference. — Mira Allende, 2026

A market for slowness

Every publishing platform that survived the contraction has the same shape underneath: a single canonical URL, a generous body type, and an author who took time to write. The fast tools — the brief generators, the carousel-makers, the auto-summarisers — survive too, but they have become tools for working on the long page, not replacements for it.

There is a class of work that cannot be done quickly. Investigative reporting, sustained argument, technical depth, the slow accumulation of a body of thought across a career — all of these need the long page to make them legible to a reader. The platforms that bet against the long page bet against this class of work, and the work continues to be done.
