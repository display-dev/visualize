The Monograph № 21
Part One · The shape of slow work
I

On the patience required to publish a single page

An essay on the cost of being heard quickly, and the work that the fast page leaves out.

For ten years I have been writing the same essay. The 2017 version was four thousand words and four citations; the version I am sending to the printer next month is twelve hundred words and one citation. It is the same essay. The shape of the work is what the years gave me — the same argument, sharpened by a decade of patience, set in ink-black on cream because the slow form is what the argument is about.

The piece is about the cost of being heard quickly, a question I first turned over in a conference handout, then in a longer piece for the Quarterly, then in a talk I gave twice and have never given again. Each time I tried to write it faster, the argument went generic. The 2017 version is a recognisable shape of think-piece prose; the 2026 version is a thing I would write only this way.1

I write this on a desk that has the same lamp it has had since I bought it secondhand in Portland in 2014. The lamp is the colour of the page I am setting — terracotta-orange, the kind you see on the unglazed underside of a flower pot. The match is not deliberate. I noticed it last week and felt the way you feel when a long pattern turns out to be a habit.

The argument of the slow essay is that returning is its own form of attention. Writing the same piece eight times across a decade is not perfectionism, which is a refusal to publish; it is the act of letting the world come back and tell you which sentences mattered.2

1.An earlier version of this argument appeared in Field Quarterly, vol. 19 (2017), p. 42. I have left the original online; the persistence of the early draft is part of the practice.
2.See M. Allende, On returning to your own out-of-print writing, Quarterly, Issue 14 (2024), pp. 88–94.
